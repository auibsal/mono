## Where we are

SAL began as a handful of students who wanted the campus to read, write and make things together. In one year it has become four programmes, a brand and a following. What it lacks is structure.

| When | What |
| --- | --- |
| [founding date] | **The Society is founded.** Shaheen Farjo starts the Society of Arts and Letters at AUIB. |
| February 2026 | **The journal brief.** A first brief, guidelines and rubric for a campus literary journal. |
| Spring 2026 | **The Typewriter Tour.** A typewriter travels the campus and collects six-word stories, with permission to publish. It proves the campus will write when invited. |
| September 2026 | **Three programmes proposed.** Second Chapter, a charity book drive for Natrok Athar; Waraq (working title), a bilingual journal; Side Quest (working title), a campus video series. Each with a full operating package. |
| October 13, 2026 | **Charter Day.** The Constitution is ratified and the Society gets the structure to carry all of this. |

**The gap this plan closes.** We have more ideas than people, more programmes than procedures, and a founder who will one day graduate. This plan, and the documents around it, turn a successful group of friends into an institution.

## Who we are

**Mission.** To cultivate a culture of literature and creative expression across AUIB.

**Vision.** A campus where every student has a place to read, write, perform and make, and a Society whose work outlasts the people who start it.

**Motto.** The paper and the pen (والقرطاسُ والقلم).

**Values**

1. **Open door.** Free, for every student, in every college, in both languages.
2. **Craft.** We take the work seriously, even when it is fun.
3. **Two languages, one page.** Arabic and English on equal footing.
4. **Care.** Consent before cameras, safety before spectacle.
5. **Every dinar visible.** Money is counted twice and reported.
6. **Built to last.** Written down, handed on, archived.

## Learning from the old societies

The best campus literary societies have lasted two centuries. We borrowed what made them last, and left behind what made them exclusive.

| Since | Society | What they do | What SAL takes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1795 | Dialectic & Philanthropic Societies, UNC, USA | Among the oldest student societies in the US. Its members built a library of over 10,000 volumes that seeded the university library, and chose a motto that still stands. | A motto; the SAL book-swap shelf; the archive left to the Library on dissolution |
| 1802 | Philolexian Society, Columbia, USA | Weekly Thursday meetings of debate and literature, the magazine Surgam, and the famous Joyce Kilmer Memorial Bad Poetry Contest. New members earn their place by taking part. | Motion Night; Bad Poetry Night; voting rights earned by taking part |
| 1848 | University Philosophical Society, "the Phil", Trinity College Dublin | Paper readings and debates every Thursday; an elected council of nine officers; medals for the best paper and speaker, and honorary patronage for distinguished guests. | A small elected Council; SAL Prizes; Honorary Members |
| 1883 | Footlights, Cambridge, UK | Informal "smokers" where anyone can try new sketches before an audience, and a path from there to full productions. | Scratch Night and the theatre ladder |
| 1911 | The Elizabethan Club, Yale, USA | Founded for students and faculty to talk about literature over tea, served every afternoon of term, beside a vault of rare books. | The Majlis, with tea, where faculty come as readers |
| 1920 | Al-Rabita al-Qalamiyya, the Pen League, New York | Gibran, Naimy and their circle met in salons and set out to renew Arabic literature, writing across two languages. | Bilingual by design; translation as a craft; the salon |
| Today | The Harvard Advocate, Harvard, USA | Boards by craft (art, fiction, poetry, features, design, business, tech) and a "comp" through which newcomers join. | Departments by craft; the three-week Apprenticeship |
| Always | Al-Mutanabbi Street and the House of Wisdom, Baghdad | A city whose book market and Abbasid-era translators are the oldest literary society we can claim. | Our motto; the name Waraq; the Mutanabbi Walk |

**What we did not borrow:** invitation-only membership, secret initiations, and fees. SAL is open, free and public, and our traditions (six words on the typewriter, the Ribbon) are ones anyone can join.

## Risks

The risks to the Society itself. Each programme keeps its own risk register.

| Risk | Level | What we do about it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The founder graduates and energy leaves with them | High | Elections in April 2027; a Founding Advisor year; handover dossiers; Year 2 Council fully post-founding |
| Too many programmes for too few people | High | Staffing by stage; the smallest working Society; the Council says no to new programmes until seats are filled |
| A few people do everything and burn out | Medium | Two roles at most; honest hours; step-back always allowed; deputies named |
| Approvals arrive late and events slip | Medium | The General Secretary as single contact; lead times in the Playbook; Plan B dates in each programme |
| Content upsets someone on a sensitive subject | Medium | Expression & Content Policy; editorial independence with clear limits; Faculty Advisor and Advisory Board |
| Money is lost or questioned | Low | Two-person cash rule; receipts in 48 h; semester Audit; charity money kept separate |
| Membership stays within one college or language | Medium | Outreach through partner clubs; Arabic and English in everything; Bad Poetry Night as a low-barrier door |
